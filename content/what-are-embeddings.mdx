When Spotify looks for your next song, one place it looks is a map. The things on it are songs, and being close can mean that people tend to put them in the same playlists. Somewhere near what you've been listening to, there might be something you'd like to hear next.

A computer looking for Jason Bourne's face in a CCTV feed could use a similar trick. Give each face a place on a map, then look for the ones close to his. But what kind of map has a place for a face? Does it have north and south?

On an ordinary map, a place has two coordinates: latitude tells us how far north or south it is, longitude how far east or west. We agree on where to start counting, at the equator and the Greenwich meridian, and two numbers are enough to find a place anywhere on Earth.

Take this ship, for example. Its crew has scurvy and needs lemons. We need to get them to the nearest port. The ship sits at `[0, 0]`, and we're measuring kilometres east and north from there. West and south get negative numbers.

Pick a port and check the distance before setting sail. For once, all the routes are clear and the wind is behaving.

The port nearest to us east-to-west isn't necessarily the nearest overall. We need both numbers. Pythagoras gives us the distance: square the difference on each axis, add the squares, then take the square root. Three squared plus four squared is twenty-five, whose square root is five. Five kilometres to the lemons.

We've flattened this little sea to keep the calculation simple. On Earth, we'd have to account for its curvature, and a degree of longitude doesn't cover the same distance everywhere. Here, two numbers per port were enough to find our destination.

Our lovely crew can get back to pillaging, conquering and spreading the word of God. All in a nice retro console look.

The colours on that console have coordinates too. In the familiar RGB system, a colour has three numbers telling the screen how much red, green and blue light to use. In the usual 8-bit representation, each channel runs from 0 to 255. Green is `[0, 255, 0]`, black is `[0, 0, 0]`, and white is `[255, 255, 255]`.

There are 256 choices for each of the three channels. Multiply them together and you get 16,777,216 combinations. On a typical screen, little red, green and blue subpixels work together to produce the colour you see. From your chair they blend into one point; up close you can see the separate lights.

Turn the red and green channels all the way up, leave blue off, and you get yellow:

```text
   Red   Green   Blue
  [255,      0,     0]  red light
+ [  0,    255,     0]  green light
  -------------------
  [255,    255,     0]  yellow
```

We're combining light here. If you try this with paint, you'll be disappointed. This combination is simple, though mixing arbitrary screen colours takes more care: the [RGB values we normally use aren't proportional to the amount of light emitted](https://www.w3.org/Graphics/Color/srgb).

Three numbers give us three axes, so we can put all these colours inside a cube. Pick a colour, then move one of its sliders and follow the point.

Black and white sit at opposite corners. Colours with similar RGB values sit near each other. We can measure the distance between them as we did with the ports, with one more difference to square and add.

This gives us a way to make our picture look older. The original Nintendo Entertainment System had a much smaller palette: 64 colour codes, several of which produced the same black. Its [colour hardware](https://www.nesdev.org/wiki/PPU_palettes) was more complicated than a box of RGB values, but we can use an RGB approximation of its palette for this experiment.

For every pixel in our image, we'll find the nearest colour the palette offers and use that instead. Press play to watch the picture being rebuilt.

The computer doesn't need to know whether a pixel belongs to a face, a tree or the sky. It compares three numbers with three other numbers and keeps the smallest distance. Repeat that for every pixel and a whole image has been redrawn.

You might disagree with some of its choices. Equal distances in RGB don't always look equally different to our eyes; [other colour spaces](https://www.w3.org/TR/css-color-4/#color-spaces) try to account for that. “Nearest” has a precise answer once we've chosen our coordinates and our way of measuring. Whether that answer looks right is another question.

A colour splits neatly into ingredients: this much red, this much green, this much blue. A song doesn't. A breakup ballad and a stadium anthem can use the same guitar, the same drums, the same voice, and still feel nothing alike. We could try placing songs between sad and happy, or between sleepy and danceable. The ends of those axes would be ideas.

Wave Race 64, Nintendo's jet ski game, puts something like this on its tuning screen. Engine runs from Dash, for quick starts, to Top End, for a higher maximum speed. Grip goes from Loose to Tight, trading speed for less slipping in turns. Handling runs from Light to Heavy, changing how far you have to push the stick to turn. The [manual explains all three](https://www.nintendo.com/eu/media/downloads/games_8/emanuals/nintendo_8/Manual_Nintendo64_WaveRace64_EN.pdf#page=5).

Which end is the good one? You pick the one that suits how you ride. You might want a quick start and sensitive steering; someone else might want speed and steadier controls. A position on these axes describes a preference. Hold on to that idea of a direction with a meaning. We'll use it again.

The four riders have different strengths. Ayumi Stewart has five stars for acceleration and two for maximum speed. Dave Mariner has the opposite ratings for those two skills. I've used the manual's ratings to give the riders approximate positions along our three axes. Set your preferences and see whose profile comes closest.

Your preferred setup gets coordinates just as the riders do, even if none of them matches it exactly. We had the same situation with the palette: you asked for a colour, and the computer found the closest one available. Here, the answer changes when your preferences move.

Football games give us far more axes. In the EA Sports FC 26 data I'm using, there are 29 detailed outfield attributes, from sprint speed and stamina to finishing and sliding tackles. Each is rated out of 99. The player card summarises them in six headline numbers: pace, shooting, passing, dribbling, defending and physical.

Six is too many for a cube. It fits on a radar chart. Choose two players and compare their shapes.

I love the spider chart. You can see the _shape_ of a player in an _Augenblick_. A winger might stretch towards pace and dribbling, a centre-back towards defending and physical. Two players with the same overall rating can have very different shapes, because that one number hides what each is good at.

Add more players and the shapes pile up into a tangle. Spread 29 attributes around the edge and it gets harder still. We could draw thousands of these, but we wouldn't learn much from looking at them.

You don't need to see a space to measure it. The calculation that got our crew to the lemons still works: take the difference on each axis, square it, add the squares up, take the square root. For a colour we used three differences. For a footballer we can use all 29. There's no step that asks us to imagine a 29-dimensional cube.

Imagine Florian Wirtz gets injured and Liverpool needs someone who plays like him. We could look through attacking midfielders one by one, or we could calculate how far every player is from Wirtz and sort the results.

For this search, I used 14,412 outfield players and rescaled each attribute according to how much it varies across that group. That gives each axis the same spread before measuring distances. A difference in an attribute where most players score alike then counts for more than the same raw difference in one where scores vary widely.

Position, club and price don't enter the calculation. Nor does the overall rating. We're comparing the 29 individual skills. Pick a candidate to see where the resemblance holds and where it breaks down.

But “almost as close” still hides differences. Olmo's stamina is 65 to Wirtz's 87; his sprint speed is 69 to Wirtz's 79. If we're trying to replace someone who runs all match, that gap matters. Giving stamina more weight would change the distances and might change our shortlist.

We've been building simple embeddings since the scurvy ship.

An embedding represents something as a list of numbers, placing it in a space where useful relationships can be measured. That list is also called a _vector_. A port got two numbers, a colour got three, a footballer got 29. In our examples, nearby points have similar coordinates, and we've used that to look for similar things.

The search has a name too: _nearest neighbours_. We've used it to find a replacement for a colour and a replacement for Wirtz. In both cases, we had to decide which differences mattered before we could ask the computer to measure them.

So far, someone has chosen every axis and given it a name. Red, grip, sprint speed. This is manageable for game characters with rating tables. How would we do it for a face? We could try measuring noses and the distance between eyes, but then we'd have to decide how to handle a head turned sideways, a different expression or bad lighting.

We can train a model to assign the coordinates for us. Show it examples of what should count as similar and let it adjust how it turns those examples into numbers. For faces, we'd want it to keep the differences that help distinguish one person from another, while ignoring a change in lighting or a new haircut.

[FaceNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03832), published in 2015, learned to represent faces with 128 numbers. Its training compared an image of a person with another image of that person and one of somebody else, rewarding distances that put the matching pair closer together. Once trained, it could give coordinates to a face it had never seen before. Our fictional Bourne-hunting computer could then compare a face from the CCTV feed with the one in its files, even when the camera angle changed. How well it learned those distinctions would determine how often it got the match wrong.

For songs, one source of training examples is the playlists people make. Spotify has [described models that learn from tracks appearing together in playlists, as well as from the audio itself](https://research.atspotify.com/2025/9/generalized-user-representations-for-large-scale-recommendations). A map learned from audio can group songs that sound alike. One learned from playlists might bring together songs that people keep choosing for the same occasions. The recommendation system can search these maps for candidates, then decide which ones to play you.

Text can be mapped too. A [sentence embedding model](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084) can put “cheap flights” near “budget airlines”, allowing a search to find related meaning when the words don't match. Many of these models compare the angle between vectors, called _cosine similarity_, instead of the straight-line distance we've used. The way of measuring changes; we're still looking for neighbours.

[Language models](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762) also begin with numerical representations of tokens, the pieces into which they split text. Those representations change as the model processes the surrounding context. “Bank” in “river bank” and “bank” in “bank account” can end up with different representations, which help the model work out what comes next.

We can still measure a learned space, but reading its coordinates is harder. Nobody has to decide that number 17 means sad or number 203 means danceable. A useful distinction might be spread across many numbers at once. The model's training determines which relationships the space preserves, just as our choice of football ratings determined which players appeared near Wirtz.

This is where the Wave Race sliders come back. A direction through a learned space can sometimes have a meaning we recognise, even if it doesn't follow any single axis. We might find that songs towards one end feel calmer and songs towards the other feel more energetic. We'd have to check the songs to know whether that reading holds. There's no printed label waiting for us at the end of the line.

We started with a map we could see and ended with spaces we can only calculate our way around. I'd still like to see where the footballers ended up.
